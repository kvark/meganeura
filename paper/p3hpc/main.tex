\begin{abstract}
Vulkan and Metal already run on nearly every consumer GPU. Can they also
carry machine-learning training and inference? \system{} is a compact
compiler and runtime, about 52k lines of Rust in a 19\,MiB executable,
that lowers tensor graphs and their gradients to Vulkan and Metal. It keeps
graph rewrites and matrix schedules in an e-graph and picks among them by
timing them on the device. We compare it with PyTorch~2.13 under
\code{torch.compile} with CUDA, HIP, and XPU graph replay on seven GPUs from
four vendors, including an MI300X reached through experimental driver
patches, and check every result numerically. In strict 32-bit floating
point, \system{}'s median time is $1.35\times$ PyTorch's for inference and
$1.61\times$ for training. It wins most comparisons on an AMD RX~7900~XT and
loses all of them on an Intel Arc~B570. Allowing reduced precision widens the
gap to $2.06\times$ and $2.21\times$: PyTorch's own times fall by a median
factor of 1.5 to 1.6, \system{}'s by 1--3\%. The same checks pass on three
integrated GPUs and a Quest~3S headset, and \system{} runs inside an Android
XR application, sharing the Vulkan queue with rendering.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

High-performance computing (HPC) uses portability layers such as Kokkos,
RAJA, and SYCL to write one program for heterogeneous hardware
\cite{edwards2014kokkos,beckingsale2019raja,khronos2020sycl}. Graphics APIs
offer another route for machine learning (ML). Vulkan runs on NVIDIA, AMD,
Intel, Qualcomm, and Arm GPUs, and Metal on Apple devices
\cite{khronos2026vulkan,apple2026metal}. An application that already renders
through these APIs can give its device, memory, and queue to a tensor runtime.
Games, simulators, and XR applications could then run ML without shipping a
second GPU stack. This paper measures what that choice gains in hardware
coverage and what it costs in speed.

\system{} is our test of the idea. It is a native compiler and runtime,
about 52k lines of Rust and 6.5k lines of WGSL shader code. It lowers a typed
tensor graph, and the training graph that automatic differentiation derives
from it, to generated compute kernels. It runs them through Blade, a thin
Vulkan and Metal layer \cite{malyshau2026barriers}. Training and inference
share the compiler, checkpoint format, and runtime on every device. The
benchmark runner that embeds all of this is a 19.1\,MiB executable
(14.6\,MiB stripped) that links only the C runtime, while the CUDA build of
PyTorch we compare against installs 4.42\,GiB of packages. Compilation can
also time alternative graphs and kernels on the target GPU before committing
to a plan. That search is optional; the main measurements enable it with a
30-second budget per session.

We compare \system{} with PyTorch~2.13 \cite{paszke2019pytorch}, compiled
with \code{torch.compile} and replayed as whole-phase CUDA, HIP, or XPU graphs
where the backend allows it. The baseline therefore avoids per-operator Python
overhead, and vendor libraries do much of its work. The main comparison
covers seven GPUs from NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, and Apple, on Linux, Windows, and
macOS, with five workloads under two arithmetic contracts. Every timed result
must first pass numerical checks. Three more integrated GPUs and the Adreno
GPU of an Android Quest~3S headset are checked against CPU references without
timing, and an Android XR application on the same headset tests deployment.
All of it comes from one collection in October 2026, at frozen compiler and
harness revisions.

PyTorch is faster in most comparisons, but the spread across platforms is
large. In strict f32, \system{} is faster in 11 of 13 comparisons on the
RX~7900~XT and in none on the Arc~B570. Reduced precision helps PyTorch far
more than \system{}: autocast moves PyTorch's forward and backward matrix
work onto the GPUs' matrix units, while \system{}'s search seldom selects
matrix kernels on NVIDIA and never on the B570, and its derivatives stay in
f32. No shipping Vulkan driver supports the MI300X; it runs the same engine
through our experimental patches to Mesa's RADV driver and to the AMDGPU
kernel driver.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A numerically checked comparison of a compact graphics-API ML stack
        with compiled PyTorch on seven GPUs from four vendors, including the
        points where each stack fails (Sections~\ref{sec:method}
        and~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item A working Vulkan compute path on MI300X through experimental Mesa
        and AMDGPU patches (Section~\ref{sec:availability}).
  \item Measurements of compile-time graph and kernel search: what it
        explores, which alternatives it selects, and what it costs
        (Section~\ref{sec:search}).
  \item Deployment evidence beyond the main matrix: Vulkan qualification on
        three integrated GPUs and a Quest~3S, models up to 1.7 billion
        parameters, and an Android XR application sharing its Vulkan queue
        with rendering (Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:edge}).
\end{itemize}
We do not measure production-scale language-model training, optimizer
updates in the timed step, distributed execution, or scientific simulation.

\system{} executes a typed, static tensor graph (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}).
Graphs come from native builders or from Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX)
and Neural Network Exchange Format (NNEF) importers. A GGUF file stores
weights and an architecture description but no graph, so an optional loader
builds the graph that the architecture implies and fills it from the file. Rewrites run in egglog,
which keeps equivalent forms of an expression in an e-graph rather than
committing to the first profitable rewrite \cite{zhang2023egglog}. For
example, the query, key, and value projections of an attention block can
stay three matrix products or become one packed product, and the e-graph
holds both. Saturation is bounded: small graphs are explored whole, larger
ones through one repeated region, such as a transformer block, that is
verified to recur. Precision changes and in-place updates end a region.
Reverse-mode differentiation builds the training graph, and an application
can add a stochastic gradient descent (SGD) or Adam update; the benchmark
leaves the update out.

Matrix schedules live in the same e-graph. Tile sizes, K staging, counted or
unrolled loops, and split-K reductions are equalities, as are transposed and
packed products. Without search, the compiler extracts one graph by estimated
tensor traffic and leaves kernel parameters to heuristics. That estimate
counts logical bytes; it has no model of caches, arithmetic, occupancy, or
launch cost. A gradient product inherits its forward schedule where that is
legal. After differentiation, search also explores independent attention
gradient layouts for queries and for keys/values. Forward graph and backward
layout choices are interleaved within the same candidate limit. Native f32
matrix and attention implementations use the cooperative tile sizes the
device exposes. Some choices, such as direct versus Winograd convolution,
still follow shape rules.

Code generation uses a few kernel families: pointwise expressions,
reductions, general matrix multiplication (GEMM), convolutions and their
derivatives, and tiled attention. Shape, device capabilities, and the
arithmetic contract decide which implementations are legal and how
workgroups are shaped. Kernels are emitted as WebGPU Shading Language (WGSL)
\cite{w3c2026wgsl}, validated by Naga, and translated by Blade into native
Vulkan or Metal pipelines. Naga is shared with wgpu; the wgpu runtime is not
used. The compiler fixes a dispatch sequence and a memory plan that reuses
buffers by lifetime. Each step records and submits that plan without
rediscovering the graph or allocating tensors, although command recording
and synchronization still happen every step. The PyTorch baseline gets the
equivalent through graph replay.

When search is enabled, every candidate is built in a private session with
representative weights and inputs. The search checks it against the untuned
ordinary build, tunes its kernels, and then races complete programs with fresh
command recording and submission. A challenger replaces the incumbent only
if it passes the numerical checks and beats it by more than the measured
noise, so graph forms compete after tuning instead of being discarded by the
cost estimate. Dispatch fusion, attention and convolution splits, and the
number of submission chunks, up to 64 per step, vary as well. The policy
measured here allows at most 16 graph and schedule forms and 64 candidate
programs in total per session, within a soft 30-second deadline and 1\,GiB
of scratch memory for kernel probes.

Kernel alternatives race each other, and a replacement kernel must be 5\%
faster; a whole-program challenger must be 1\% faster and clear the measured
noise. The selected program then runs and is checked twice more. Kernel
decisions persist within each campaign, model, and profile, so later
processes can reuse them, but every process searches whole programs afresh.

\section{Checking the Compiler and Its Kernels}
\label{sec:validation}

Comparing two frameworks is useful, but when they disagree it does not say
which one is wrong, and a deterministic indexing bug repeats perfectly. \system{}'s test suite therefore checks the compiler
against an independent reference in three
ways.\footnote{\href{https://github.com/kvark/meganeura/tree/c19cb1caedd90be7abc61de61f6197018711398e/src/reference}{Reference interpreter and checks at the measured revision}.}

First, a small CPU interpreter evaluates every graph operator in 64-bit
floating point and shares no lowering or shader code with the GPU path.
Inputs are rounded to their storage precision first. Symbolic derivatives
evaluated by the interpreter are compared with central finite differences.
Nonuniform weighted losses and directional probes ensure that gradients are
tested beyond an all-ones upstream derivative.

Second, operator sweeps compare every GPU output and gradient element with the
interpreter, on shapes chosen to hit tile edges, reduction splits, and
alternative lowerings. The tolerance scales per element and uses sums of
absolute products for reductions prone to cancellation. It is a practical
error model rather than a proof. Third, composed blocks and seeded random
graphs pass through differentiation, e-graph optimization, dispatch fusion,
and memory planning. Their outputs and gradients, including gradients that
the optimizer packed, are compared with the unoptimized reference. Storage
starts filled with NaNs, so a missing write or an invalid buffer reuse shows
up.

These checks found real bugs: parameter gradients dropped during fusion, loss
reductions that were not scalar, cancellation in normalization, and vanishing
GELU tails. In the DinoVision application (Section~\ref{sec:edge}), a
LayerScale layout error updated only the first token while the output images
still looked plausible, and only an independent reference caught it. Adding
an operator to the IR requires either a reference implementation or an
explicit unsupported result; packed quantized formats are unsupported. None
of this covers every shape, schedule, driver, or device, so the benchmark
adds its own gates (Section~\ref{sec:gates}).

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\inferena{}, our open-source cross-framework harness, builds matched
workloads, alternates engine order, synchronizes timing boundaries, and
records raw samples, outputs, gradient norms, revisions, and environment
metadata.\footnote{\href{https://github.com/kvark/inferena/tree/add19182e5479e3f7549894c524ac6691d3dc363}{Inferena source, revision \code{add19182}}.}
It supplies kernels to neither engine.

\subsection{Revisions and reference policy}
\label{sec:reference-policy}

All runs use \inferena{} \code{add19182}, \system{} \code{c19cb1c}, Blade
\code{4845d65}, and Naga \code{13f582d}. Every PyTorch wheel reports version 2.13.0 at source
commit \code{cf30153}, with Python 3.13.13: \code{+cu130} for NVIDIA,
\code{+rocm7.2} for AMD, \code{+xpu} for Arc, and the macOS wheel with MPS
for Apple. Drivers, host CPUs, and vendor libraries still differ between
platforms, so a common PyTorch revision does not make the backends identical.

PyTorch uses \code{torch.compile} with default settings. Max-autotune,
GEMM and pointwise autotuning, and coordinate-descent tuning are off. The
compiler's and vendor libraries' own heuristics still apply
\cite{pytorch2026compile}. We did not use max-autotune because it was costly
and unreliable during bring-up. It compiled for a long time on H100, its
convolution searches failed on both Radeons, and it did not finish its first
ResNet check on Arc within an hour.\footnote{\href{https://github.com/kvark/inferena/blob/add19182e5479e3f7549894c524ac6691d3dc363/EXPERIMENT.md}{Inferena protocol and bring-up notes, revision \code{add19182}}.}
A supervisor gives compilation 120 seconds for the first forward, backward,
and minimal-shape calls, and terminates the compiler on timeout. The engines
therefore prepare under different budgets. This is neither an equal-budget
contest nor a comparison with the best PyTorch result after hours of search.

CUDA and ROCm replay each phase through \path{torch.cuda.CUDAGraph}, which
ROCm maps to HIP graphs, and Intel through \path{torch.xpu.XPUGraph}
\cite{pytorch2026hip,pytorch2026xpugraph}. Inductor's own CUDA-graph
mechanism is disabled, so the explicit capture is the only replay. XPU
capture needs two recorded accommodations: the math scaled-dot-product
attention (SDPA) backend and an equivalent formulation of embedding backward
(Section~\ref{sec:availability}). MPS compiles every phase but has no public
whole-phase replay \cite{pytorch2026mpssource}. No failed compiled or
captured phase falls back to eager or CPU execution.

Each process times three phases: a forward pass without gradients at the
full shape, a forward pass at a minimal shape, and forward--loss--backward
(F+L+B) without an optimizer update. For SmolLM2, the full forward is a
128-token prefill, and the minimal one is a single token without a key/value
cache, so it is not decode latency. The diffusion U-Net and Whisper already
run at batch one, so their minimal and full shapes coincide. Each phase warms
up for at least five calls and ten seconds, then records 20 synchronized
host-wall samples. Input upload and output readback happen outside the timed
interval.

Each condition runs in three fresh process pairs, with alternating engine
order and rotating condition order. We report the median of the three
per-process medians and keep every sample and the range. A phase enters an
aggregate only with three qualified pairs, where \emph{qualified} means it
passed the gates of Section~\ref{sec:gates}. A failed backward pass does not
discard a qualified forward pass, and no eager run or older result fills a
missing one.

\subsection{Arithmetic contracts}
\label{sec:contracts}

Strict f32 disables TensorFloat-32 (TF32) in PyTorch and reduced-precision
matrix inputs in both engines. Storage, accumulation, and outputs stay f32.
Matrix tiles with f32 inputs and f32 accumulation remain allowed, because
they keep the arithmetic width, though not the reduction order. The M3
exposes such $8\times8\times8$ tiles and the MI300X $16\times16\times16$
ones; the other Vulkan GPUs expose none.

The accelerated contract runs PyTorch under autocast, with BF16 on every
CUDA, HIP, and XPU GPU here and FP16 on MPS. The dtype is fixed per model and
backend before compilation and is not retried after a numerical failure.
One declared exception keeps SmolLM2-135M's one-token phase in f32; the
larger models use autocast there too. Parameters stay f32, operations left in
f32 keep TF32 off, and FP16 training includes GradScaler's scaling,
unscaling, finite checks, and scale update in the timed step. \system{}
keeps tensors and accumulation in f32 and converts inside each step. Its
search may choose f16 matrix kernels or BF16 matrix inputs for forward
regions, including rectangular subgroup tiles and NVIDIA's workgroup-scope
tiles where the device exposes them, or keep the f32 program. Derivative
regions stay f32, whereas PyTorch's autocast also covers the backward pass,
so the two engines do different arithmetic under this contract.

\subsection{Numerical gates}
\label{sec:gates}

The gates decide which implementations are eligible, in both engines and
during \system{}'s search, so wider bounds could admit faster programs. We
did not measure that sensitivity, and the two engines' procedures differ.

\emph{Native fidelity.} Before precision conversion or search, an untuned
build of the original graph supplies a strict-f32 full-tensor reference.
Strict candidates must satisfy maximum- and RMS-error bounds of
$10^{-6}+10^{-4}r$, where $r$ is the corresponding reference scale.
Accelerated outputs of the non-language models use a coefficient of 0.02, and
the full training-gradient vector 0.01. Accelerated language-model inference
is gated on the output distribution instead: mean token KL divergence at most
$\log(1.01)$, worst-token KL at most $\log(1.10)$, and mean loss deviation at
most $10^{-6}+0.02|L_{\rm ref}|$. The two final executions of the selected
program must repeat within the same bounds, and a rejected candidate never
replaces a qualified incumbent.

\emph{PyTorch fidelity and training.} The accelerated forward pass is checked
against an eager strict-f32 run with the same output or distribution bounds.
Training must also follow a matched SGD trajectory: 256 updates on frozen,
varying real-text batches for every SmolLM2 size, with probe and held-out
checkpoints, or 32 repeated-input updates for the other models. Losses must
stay within 2\% plus $10^{-6}$, and every update must finish with finite
values. These checks are untimed and restore state before timing. \system{}'s
gate checks full single-step gradients instead of the same 256-step
trajectory, so neither gate substitutes for the other.

\emph{Repeatability.} PyTorch compares all output and gradient elements
across two uncaptured calls (eight for accelerated training), and across two
graph replays when capture is used. Output and strict-gradient bounds are
$10^{-6}+10^{-4}r$; accelerated gradients use a whole-vector coefficient of
0.01.

\emph{Cross-engine comparison.} Sampled outputs (256 positions) and the loss
must agree within 1\%, and the total gradient norm and the vector of
parameter norms within 5\% for strict. Accelerated allows 10\% per process
but requires the three-process median below 5\%. Packed gradients are
restored to checkpoint coordinates, and accelerated comparisons use the
strict-f32 snapshots that both engines preserve. Samples and norms can miss
local or sign errors, which is why the full native checks exist.

When a compiled reference fails, its phase has no ratio, although the
preserved snapshots or a checked eager diagnostic can still qualify the
native result. A rejection means that the declared contract failed; it does
not by itself show a defect in a vendor stack. Because \system{} searches
among qualified alternatives while PyTorch uses one fixed dtype policy,
coverage also reflects the two preparation policies.

\subsection{Performance}
\label{sec:performance}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} gives every ratio, and Fig.~\ref{fig:ratios}
plots the strict ones. Across the seven platforms, the median strict ratio is
1.35 for inference, 1.03 for the minimal shape, and 1.61 for training.
\system{} is faster in 10 of 35, 15 of 33, and 4 of 33 comparisons. Four of
the fifteen minimal-shape wins repeat inference wins, because diffusion's and
Whisper's shapes coincide. Under the accelerated contract, the medians are
2.06, 1.49, and 2.21, with 4, 13, and 4 wins out of 32 each.

The platforms differ more than these medians suggest. On the RX~7900~XT,
\system{} is faster in 11 of 13 strict comparisons; SmolLM2's prefill,
one-token forward, and training take 0.61, 0.19, and 0.67 of PyTorch's time.
On the Arc~B570, PyTorch wins every phase under both contracts, with strict
inference ratios of 1.62--5.45 and training ratios of 1.86--3.50; SmolLM2
training takes 50.83\,ms against PyTorch's 17.40\,ms strict and 12.59\,ms
accelerated. On the RTX~5070, strict inference ratios span 0.74--1.56 and
training ratios 1.24--1.95, and the Windows RTX~3050 is similar, at
0.97--1.51 and 1.29--1.69. The H100 spans 0.94--2.24 and 1.27--2.94, and the
MI300X 1.72--3.14 and 1.99--3.25.

The accelerated contract helps PyTorch far more than \system{}. Over the
cells where each engine qualified under both contracts, it cuts PyTorch's
median time by a factor of 1.50 for inference and 1.56 for training, and
\system{}'s by 1.03 and 1.01. Per GPU, over the workloads where both engines
qualified, PyTorch's median gain is 1.22--3.12 on the NVIDIA GPUs, the
RX~7900~XT, and the B570, where autocast moves its matrix work in both
directions onto the matrix units. On the H100, accelerated SmolLM2 training
takes 17.49\,ms in \system{} and 4.77\,ms in PyTorch. On the MI300X and M3,
PyTorch gains little or slows down, with median gains of 0.76 and 1.08 for
inference and 0.85 and 0.69 for training. \system{} already uses f32 matrix
tiles there under the strict contract, so these two GPUs' ratios improve.

We keep every process and sample. The spread of an engine's three process
medians, $(\max t-\min t)/\operatorname{median}(t)$, has a median of at most
2.1\% on every GPU but the M3. There it is 33.8\%, and 59 of 60 phase groups
exceed 10\% despite sleep prevention. We have no clock or power trace to
explain it, so the M3 ratios support only broad conclusions. Seven of 60 B570
groups exceed 10\%, up to 27.1\%, all of them \system{}'s, and two of 58 on
the RX~7900~XT, both PyTorch's. On the H100, one native accelerated diffusion
minimal-shape process took 5.310\,ms against 1.773 and 1.772\,ms for the
other two, with the same selected program; that phase has no PyTorch
comparison anyway. The CSV keeps every process median.

\subsection{Where the time goes}
\label{sec:gaps}

Host-wall time includes command recording, submission, waiting, and GPU
execution, and this collection has no device timeline to divide a gap among
them. The search receipts do show how much matrix work each selected program
has. Under the strict contract, only the M3 and MI300X programs use matrix
tiles, in up to 513 and 543 dispatches per program. Under the accelerated
contract, the selected NVIDIA programs use matrix kernels in at most 19
dispatches, all in forward regions. Six of 42 sessions on each NVIDIA GPU
reuse workgroup-scope kernel decisions through
\code{VK\_NV\_cooperative\_matrix2} \cite{khronos2024cooperative2}, and BF16
is selected in three sessions each on the RTX~5070 and RTX~3050 and in none
on the H100. The B570 advertises rectangular $8\times16\times16$ f16 and
BF16 tiles, which the compiler accepts, yet none of its 42 accelerated
sessions selects a matrix dispatch. The MI300X's Vulkan driver exposes f16
and f32 tiles but no BF16, while ROCm PyTorch uses BF16. So the matrix
extensions that the H100 and B570 do expose are rarely used: the search
seldom selects the matrix kernels it has, and derivatives never use them.

Other hardware paths stay out of reach of Vulkan shaders altogether. Hopper's CUDA
path issues asynchronous matrix operations across a 128-thread warpgroup
(\code{wgmma}) \cite{nvidia2026wgmma}; these, thread-block clusters, and
TF32 matrix inputs are all unavailable through this stack. Equal extension
support would still not imply equal instructions, conversion traffic,
occupancy, or fusion. Separating these factors would take matched-kernel
inspection or feature-off runs, which this collection does not include.

Performance portability requires the code to run first. The three
integrated GPUs and the Quest~3S's Adreno~740 pass all 30 cells each against

ROCm setups failed on both Radeon iGPUs, and the pinned PyTorch found no XPU

only the receipts and their hashes.

The missing PyTorch references have different causes. On the RX~7900~XT,
strict Whisper fails training-output repeatability in all three processes;
on the MI300X, strict ResNet fails gradient repeatability. The minimal-shape
phase, which runs after training, is then not attempted. MI300X accelerated
diffusion and Whisper fail repeatability or forward fidelity. H100
accelerated diffusion exceeds the maximum forward-error bound (0.02255
against 0.02085) while its RMS error passes. H100 and MI300X accelerated
1.7B exceed the worst-token KL bound (about 0.1073 and 0.1030 against
$\log(1.10)\simeq0.09531$) while mean KL passes. These are failures of the
declared contract; we have not traced them to driver or library defects.

The B570 360M campaign failed differently. In the first strict PyTorch
process, compiled backward capture throws ``wait method cannot be used for
an event associated with a command graph'' from below an Inductor-generated
operator in the XPU/SYCL path. Our harness does not classify this message as
recoverable, so it stopped the campaign. \system{} completed the condition
and its internal checks, but without a reference or replication its six
cells are excluded, and no earlier uncaptured diagnostic replaces them. The
small-model XPU path keeps its math-SDPA and embedding-backward
accommodations.

The MI300X is a compute-only accelerator, and Mesa's RADV driver supports
only graphics-capable AMD GPUs \cite{mesa2026radv}. Building on Mesa's
experimental CDNA work \cite{mesa2025cdna}, we extended RADV with
buffer-based compute and cooperative matrices, and patched the AMDGPU kernel
driver with an interface through which RADV coordinates work across the
GPU's eight XCC compute dies. With these patches, the same \system{} and
\inferena{} revisions as on the other platforms qualify all 42 native cells,
up to SmolLM2-1.7B. The recorded device is \emph{AMD Instinct MI300X VF (RADV
GFX940)}, with 304 compute units and about 192\,GiB of memory. General image
support and Vulkan conformance are outside this work.

The runs load Mesa built from commit \code{8c7f5fdd}, the head of a draft
merge request \cite{malyshau2026mi300}, on Linux \code{6.8.0-146-generic}.
The kernel patch, attached to the same request, applies to AMD's vendor
driver source \code{amdgpu-6.19.14-2364437.24.04}; the module is loaded with
\code{mi300\_xcc\_gang=1} and a 60-second compute lockup timeout. The
reference needs one accommodation as well:
\path{MIOPEN_DEBUG_CONV_IMPLICIT_GEMM_ASM_FWD_GTC_XDLOPS_NHWC=0} excludes a
convolution solver that had failed earlier. The kernel settings and this
override are recorded in the provenance of earlier runs on the same machine
with the same Mesa build; the final archives record only the Mesa build and
the host kernel. Both stacks are thus explicitly configured, and neither
stock installation is shown to work without intervention.

\subsection{What it explores}

The 588 core sessions measured 23,669 whole programs, an average of 40 per
session against a limit of 64 (Table~\ref{tab:search}). In 306 sessions
(52\%), the chosen graph or schedule differs from ordinary extraction, so the
retained alternatives do get used. The search is budget-bound: in 428
sessions (73\%), the deadline arrived before every program was measured.
Thirteen programs were rejected for numerical mismatch, and 34 trials stopped
at the probe scratch limit; a rejected candidate never replaces the checked
incumbent. The M3 kept the extracted graph in 63 of its 84 sessions, which is
consistent with its noisy timing, since a challenger must beat the incumbent
by more than the measured noise. Device features only supply candidates, as
the B570 and BF16 selections in Section~\ref{sec:gaps} show.

A workload builds three sessions, or two for Whisper. The 30-second deadline
is soft, and preparing the original-f32 reference falls outside it but inside
the native preparation time. This cohort has no search-off, feature-off, or
alternate-budget arm, so it does not measure what the search gains.
Historical search studies used other revisions and policies.

\section{Deployment Beyond the Matrix}
\label{sec:edge}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\columnwidth]{../figures/dinovision-quest3s.jpg}
  \caption{DinoVision's live RGB reconstruction on a Quest~3S, captured
  from the headset's cast.}
  \label{fig:dinovision}
\end{figure}

The case that motivates a graphics-API ML stack is an application that
already renders. DinoVision is one such application. It runs on the
Qualcomm Adreno GPU of a Meta Quest~3S under Android and reconstructs the
headset cameras' images from features of a DINOv3 vision encoder
\cite{simeoni2025dinov3} (Fig.~\ref{fig:dinovision}). \system{} trains the
2.01-million-parameter decoder on a desktop GPU with Adam and saves the
checkpoint. The headset loads it into a joined batch-one encoder/decoder
graph. The deployed graph performs 2.359 billion multiply--accumulates and
uses the same graph representation, compiler, memory planner, checkpoint
format, and Blade runtime as the benchmark. Two asynchronous eye sessions
share Blade's Vulkan context and queue with the OpenXR renderer.

We checked two things. First, for one fixed public RGB frame and identical
weights, the preprocessed patches are bit-exact between desktop and headset.
The encoder output agrees to a relative L2 error of 0.001059, and the final
reconstruction to 0.000457. Second, sharing the queue changes what the right
schedule is. In isolation, the graph runs in 96.78\,ms as one submission, and
splitting it into two chunks costs 4.1\%. During a 90-second live stereo
session, the two-chunk plan instead raises the application's render
submissions by 18.8\% and the slower eye's update rate by 8.7\%, and cuts
median worker latency by 20.2\%. Larger chunk counts help less, and
unevenly. The best plan for the renderer is not the best in isolation, and
one shared graphics stack lets the application choose it. This is a
deployment check, not an on-device training or PyTorch-on-Android
comparison, and it predates the framework cohort with its own pinned
revisions. The weights, raw samples, manifests, and full-resolution media
are in the public
\href{https://huggingface.co/mad-bot/dinovision/tree/2c3f9017fe74c41482b165890c14737a2ccd4b6a}{DinoVision evidence archive},
and its \href{https://github.com/kvark/dinovision/tree/dc35cdf1c7c910cdd93c5b5362846842ae469a21}{source code}
is public too.

\section{Maintenance and Debugging}
\label{sec:productivity}

We did not measure developer effort, learnability, or maintenance time, and
we claim no quantitative productivity advantage. Some observations still
bear on it. Application code expresses tensor graphs and reuses checkpoints
across backends. One stack means one implementation to maintain, but
maintaining it takes WGSL, memory, synchronization, and numerical expertise.
A static compiler can fuse away or alias the values a developer wants to
inspect, so debugging may need a less optimized plan. \system{} can read
named intermediates, map graph operations to dispatches, locate the first
nonfinite value, and run the same kernels eagerly. These tools separate
rewrite, storage-reuse, and kernel bugs, although eager execution of the same
kernels is not an independent reference. Calibrated timestamps align CPU work
with GPU passes, while native tools such as Nsight Graphics remain necessary
for occupancy and stall analysis \cite{nvidia2026gputrace}. Deployment needs
no Python or vendor ML runtime, which kept the DinoVision integration within
one graphics stack.

\section{Related Work}

Pennycook et al.\ define and later revisit the portability metric used here
\cite{pennycook2016metric,pennycook2021revisiting}. Kokkos and RAJA express
portable parallelism and memory policy in C++ libraries
\cite{edwards2014kokkos,beckingsale2019raja}, and SYCL standardizes
single-source heterogeneous C++~\cite{khronos2020sycl}. We start instead from
graphics APIs and build a tensor compiler on top. Lin et al.\ measure
programming-model productivity through semantic divergence
\cite{lin2024productivity}; preparation time and executable size do not
replace that measure.

TVM, Glow, MLIR, and Triton established graph and kernel compilation for ML
\cite{chen2018tvm,rotem2018glow,lattner2021mlir,tillet2019triton}, and RAF
extends whole-graph compilation to training~\cite{yu2023raf}. TASO searches
verified graph substitutions \cite{jia2019taso}. TENSAT and Glenside apply
equality saturation to tensor programs
\cite{yang2021tensat,smith2021glenside}, building on egg
\cite{willsey2021egg}. \system{} uses egglog \cite{zhang2023egglog}, adds
matrix schedules to the same e-graph, and measures candidates instead of
trusting one cost model. Compile-time autotuning is itself established
practice \cite{chen2018tvm,tillet2019triton}.

Several systems already run ML through graphics APIs. IREE compiles MLIR
programs to Vulkan and Metal, and TinyIREE measures its footprint for
embedded inference \cite{liu2022tinyiree}. llama.cpp \cite{ggml2026llamacpp}
and ncnn \cite{tencent2026ncnn} run inference through Vulkan kernels, WebLLM
and LlamaWeb do so through WebGPU \cite{ruan2024webllm,levine2026llamaweb},
and ExecuTorch deploys exported PyTorch programs across mobile backends
\cite{nachin2026executorch}. TensorFlow.js trained models in the browser
\cite{smilkov2019tensorflowjs}, and memory-constrained on-device training
studies a complementary regime \cite{lin2022ondevice}. Burn is a broader Rust
framework with compute and graphics backends, autodiff, and fusion
\cite{simard2026burn}. Our contribution is a measured comparison of one
compact graphics-API stack for both training and inference against compiled
PyTorch across vendors, including its failures, preparation cost, and a
deployment. \inferena{} can drive several of these frameworks, but we compare
only against PyTorch, which trains on the GPUs of all four vendors in the
timed comparison.

\section{Threats to Validity}

\emph{Platforms.} A common PyTorch revision does not equalize vendor
libraries, drivers, host CPUs, or operating systems. The XPU accommodations
and the MI300X solver exclusion change the reference paths, the MI300X
native path needs experimental driver patches, and MPS has no whole-phase
replay. The results apply to these recorded configurations.

\emph{Budgets.} The engines prepare under different policies and limits, so
we claim neither equal startup cost nor the fastest possible implementation
of either.

\emph{Replication.} Three processes per condition do not characterize a
population of machines. CPU power management, cloud co-tenancy, thermals,
and the short timing window can all matter. Fresh processes start with empty
TorchInductor caches but keep whatever driver state the machine had. We
report warmed short-window latency rather than sustained throughput and
remove no outliers.

\emph{Numerics.} Strict f32 fixes arithmetic width, not operation order.
Native full-gradient checks, PyTorch's trajectory gate, and the cross-engine
snapshots have different scopes (Section~\ref{sec:gates}), and none
establishes equal learning quality. The gates also select: wider bounds could
change native kernel selection as well as PyTorch's eligibility, and
relabeling archived failures cannot reconstruct that experiment. Aggregates
cover only the cells where both engines qualified, and the portability
metric, which mixes coverage with speed, inherits the numerical policy.

\emph{Scope.} The larger models run at batch one with short contexts and no
key/value cache. The timed step excludes optimizer updates and communication,
and there is no distributed training experiment. The diffusion U-Net is
reduced, Whisper lacks its decoder, and ResNet folds its normalization.
Experience with several vendors shaped this compiler, so the results do not
separate its architecture from the engineering effort behind it.

\section{Future Work}

The gaps point at the compiler's next steps: matrix kernels for backward
passes and convolutions, broader BF16 coverage, and a cheaper search. The
B570 needs rectangular matrix kernels that win, and the H100 needs
matched-precision kernel and memory analysis, since enabling the features
has not closed either gap. The next experiments should isolate the effects
of search budget, kernel-decision reuse, matrix features, and numerical
bounds on the same workloads, and native trajectory checks would remove an
asymmetry in qualification. The MI300X driver work needs further validation
and upstream review. Further out, persistent megakernels could move
dependency scheduling onto the GPU, as MPK does for CUDA task graphs
\cite{cheng2026mpk}, subject to graphics-API forward-progress and
synchronization constraints.

\section{Conclusion}

Graphics APIs are a workable path for ML deployment. \system{}, about 52k
lines of Rust in a 19\,MiB executable, trains and runs five workloads on
eleven GPUs from five vendors under Linux, Windows, macOS, and Android,
including an MI300X with experimental driver patches, and runs inside an
Android XR application next to its renderer. PyTorch is still faster in most
comparisons. \system{}'s median strict time is $1.35\times$ PyTorch's for
inference and $1.61\times$ for training, with wins on the RX~7900~XT and
losses everywhere on the Arc~B570. Reduced precision widens the gap to
$2.06\times$ and $2.21\times$, because PyTorch moves its matrix work in both
directions onto matrix units, while \system{} seldom selects matrix kernels
and keeps derivatives in f32. Larger models do not settle the question:
MI300X's strict training ratio narrows to 1.65 at 1.7B, while H100's grows
to 3.79. Both stacks also have coverage gaps, so availability belongs beside
speed when judging portability.
