\begin{abstract}
Vulkan and Metal already run on nearly every consumer GPU. Can they also
carry machine-learning training and inference? \system{} is a compact
compiler and runtime, about 42k lines of Rust in a 13.5\,MiB executable,
that lowers tensor graphs and their gradients to Vulkan and Metal. It keeps
graph rewrites and matrix schedules in an e-graph and picks among them by
timing them on the device. We compare it with PyTorch~2.13 under
\code{torch.compile} with CUDA, HIP, and XPU graph replay, on six GPUs from
four vendors, and check every result numerically. In strict 32-bit floating
point, \system{}'s median time is $1.27\times$ PyTorch's for inference and
$1.92\times$ for training. It wins most comparisons on an AMD RX~7900~XT and
loses all of them on an Intel Arc~B570. It also passes the same checks on
three integrated GPUs where our PyTorch setups had no working GPU path, and
runs inside an Android XR application, sharing the Vulkan queue with
rendering; it cannot run on an MI300X, which lacks a Vulkan driver.
Allowing reduced precision widens the gap to $2.00\times$ and $2.61\times$.
On two GPUs, its measured search is $1.18$--$1.55\times$ faster than no search
on average, against 2--10\% for a two-second tuner, but it raises preparation
from about two seconds to about two minutes per workload.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

High-performance computing (HPC) uses portability layers such as Kokkos,
RAJA, and SYCL to write one program for heterogeneous hardware
\cite{edwards2014kokkos,beckingsale2019raja,khronos2020sycl}. Graphics APIs
offer another route for machine learning (ML). Vulkan runs on NVIDIA, AMD,
Intel, Qualcomm, and Arm GPUs, and Metal on Apple devices
\cite{khronos2026vulkan,apple2026metal}. An application that already renders
through these APIs can give its device, memory, and queue to a tensor runtime.
Games, simulators, and XR applications could then run ML without shipping a
second GPU stack. This paper measures what that choice gains in hardware
coverage and what it costs in speed.

\system{} is our test of the idea. It is a native compiler and runtime,
about 42k lines of Rust and 4.7k lines of WGSL shader code. It lowers a typed
tensor graph, and the training graph that automatic differentiation derives
from it, to generated compute kernels. It runs them through Blade, a thin
Vulkan and Metal layer \cite{malyshau2026barriers}. Training and inference
share the compiler, checkpoint format, and runtime on every device. The
benchmark runner that embeds all of this is a 13.5\,MiB executable that
links only the C runtime, while the CUDA build of PyTorch we compare against
installs 4.41\,GiB of packages. Compilation can also time alternative graphs
and kernels on the target GPU before committing to a plan. That search is
optional; the main measurements enable it with a 60-second budget per
session.

We compare \system{} with PyTorch~2.13 \cite{paszke2019pytorch}, compiled
with \code{torch.compile} and replayed as whole-phase CUDA, HIP, or XPU graphs
where the backend allows it. The baseline therefore avoids per-operator Python
overhead, and vendor libraries do much of its work. The main comparison
covers six GPUs from NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, and Apple, on Linux, Windows, and
macOS, with five workloads under two arithmetic contracts. Every timed result
must first pass numerical checks. Three more integrated GPUs are checked
against a CPU reference, and an Android XR application on a Qualcomm Adreno
GPU tests deployment.

PyTorch is faster in most comparisons, but the spread across platforms is
large. \system{} wins most comparisons on the RX~7900~XT and loses every one
on the Arc~B570. On three integrated GPUs, our PyTorch setups had no working
GPU path while \system{} ran and passed. On an MI300X, the reverse holds:
there is no Vulkan driver. The measured search matters. On the RTX~5070 and
Arc~B570, it makes \system{} $1.18$--$1.55\times$ faster than no search on
average across workloads, and up to $2.13\times$ in one phase, while the
library's simpler two-second tuner gains only 2--10\%. It also raises
preparation from about two seconds to about two minutes per workload.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A numerically checked comparison of a compact graphics-API ML stack
        with compiled PyTorch on six GPUs from four vendors, including the
        points where each stack fails (Sections~\ref{sec:method}
        and~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item Measurements of compile-time graph and kernel search: what it
        explores, what it costs, and what it gains over no search and over
        a two-second tuner at the same revision (Section~\ref{sec:search}).
  \item Deployment evidence beyond the main matrix: Vulkan qualification on
        three integrated GPUs, models up to 1.7 billion parameters, and an
        Android XR application sharing its Vulkan queue with rendering
        (Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:edge}).
\end{itemize}
We do not measure production-scale language-model training, optimizer
updates in the timed step, distributed execution, or scientific simulation.

\system{} executes a typed, static tensor graph (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}).
Graphs come from native builders or from Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX)
and Neural Network Exchange Format (NNEF) importers. A GGUF file stores
weights and an architecture description but no graph, so an optional loader
builds the graph that the architecture implies and fills it from the file. Rewrites run in egglog,
which keeps equivalent forms of an expression in an e-graph rather than
committing to the first profitable rewrite \cite{zhang2023egglog}. For
example, the query, key, and value projections of an attention block can
stay three matrix products or become one packed product, and the e-graph
holds both. Saturation is bounded: small graphs are explored whole, larger
ones through one repeated region, such as a transformer block, that is
verified to recur. Precision changes and in-place updates end a region.
Reverse-mode differentiation builds the training graph, and an application
can add a stochastic gradient descent (SGD) or Adam update; the benchmark
leaves the update out.

Matrix schedules live in the same e-graph. Tile sizes, K staging, counted or
unrolled loops, and split-K reductions are equalities, as are transposed and
packed products. Without search, the compiler extracts one graph by estimated
tensor traffic and leaves kernel parameters to heuristics. That estimate
counts logical bytes; it has no model of caches, arithmetic, occupancy, or
launch cost. A gradient product inherits its forward schedule where that is
legal. Independent gradient schedules and cooperative tile shapes are not
searched yet, and some choices, such as direct versus Winograd convolution,
still follow shape rules.

Code generation uses a few kernel families: pointwise expressions,
reductions, general matrix multiplication (GEMM), convolutions and their
derivatives, and tiled attention. Shape, device capabilities, and the
arithmetic contract decide which implementations are legal and how
workgroups are shaped. Kernels are emitted as WebGPU Shading Language (WGSL)
\cite{w3c2026wgsl}, validated by Naga, and translated by Blade into native
Vulkan or Metal pipelines. Naga is shared with wgpu; the wgpu runtime is not
used. The compiler fixes a dispatch sequence and a memory plan that reuses
buffers by lifetime. Each step records and submits that plan without
rediscovering the graph or allocating tensors, although command recording
and synchronization still happen every step. The PyTorch baseline gets the
equivalent through graph replay.

When search is enabled, every candidate is built in a private session with
representative weights and inputs. The search checks it against the untuned
ordinary build, tunes its kernels, and then races complete programs with fresh
command recording and submission. A challenger replaces the incumbent only
if it passes the numerical checks and beats it by more than the measured
noise, so graph forms compete after tuning instead of being discarded by the
cost estimate. Dispatch fusion, attention and convolution splits, and the
number of submission chunks vary as well. The policy measured here allows at
most 16 graph and schedule forms and 64 candidate programs in total per
session, within a soft 60-second deadline and 1\,GiB of scratch memory for
kernel probes.

The search did not start this large. It began as the library's optional
tuner, which tunes kernel parameters on the extracted graph for up to two
seconds. Finding better matrix tilings meant widening its scope to whole
programs, and moving to egglog~3 put graph forms and schedules into one
search. Where the budget should stop is still open;
Section~\ref{sec:search} measures what the two-second tuner and the full
search each deliver at the measured revision.

\section{Checking the Compiler and Its Kernels}
\label{sec:validation}

Comparing two frameworks is useful, but when they disagree it does not say
which one is wrong, and a deterministic indexing bug repeats perfectly. \system{}'s test suite therefore checks the compiler
against an independent reference in three
ways.\footnote{\href{https://github.com/kvark/meganeura/tree/0dbfcc0029bf98b33a03fc792e1f4e90ead17f23/src/reference}{Reference interpreter and checks at the measured revision}.}

First, a small CPU interpreter evaluates every graph operator in 64-bit
floating point and shares no lowering or shader code with the GPU path.
Inputs are rounded to their storage precision first. Symbolic derivatives
evaluated by the interpreter are compared with central finite differences.
Nonuniform weighted losses and directional probes ensure that gradients are
tested beyond an all-ones upstream derivative.

Second, operator sweeps compare every GPU output and gradient element with the
interpreter, on shapes chosen to hit tile edges, reduction splits, and
alternative lowerings. The tolerance scales per element and uses sums of
absolute products for reductions prone to cancellation. It is a practical
error model rather than a proof. Third, composed blocks and seeded random
graphs pass through differentiation, e-graph optimization, dispatch fusion,
and memory planning. Their outputs and gradients, including gradients that
the optimizer packed, are compared with the unoptimized reference. Storage
starts filled with NaNs, so a missing write or an invalid buffer reuse shows
up.

These checks found real bugs: parameter gradients dropped during fusion, loss
reductions that were not scalar, cancellation in normalization, and vanishing
GELU tails. In the DinoVision application (Section~\ref{sec:edge}), a
LayerScale layout error updated only the first token while the output images
still looked plausible, and only an independent reference caught it. Adding
an operator to the IR requires either a reference implementation or an
explicit unsupported result; packed quantized formats are unsupported. None
of this covers every shape, schedule, driver, or device, so the benchmark
adds its own gates (Section~\ref{sec:gates}).

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\inferena{}, our open-source cross-framework harness, builds matched
workloads, alternates engine order, synchronizes timing boundaries, and
records raw samples, outputs, gradient norms, revisions, and environment
metadata.\footnote{\href{https://github.com/kvark/inferena/tree/7b8fcb72e55410d8e33bbe948f89be50c691fcf7}{Inferena source, revision \code{7b8fcb72}}.}
It supplies kernels to neither engine.

\subsection{Revisions and reference policy}
\label{sec:reference-policy}

All runs use \inferena{} \code{7b8fcb72}, \system{} \code{0dbfcc00}, and
Blade \code{fbb4f28}. Every PyTorch wheel reports version 2.13.0 at source
commit \code{cf30153}, with Python 3.13.13: \code{+cu130} for NVIDIA,
\code{+rocm7.2} for AMD, \code{+xpu} for Arc, and the macOS wheel with MPS
for Apple. Drivers, host CPUs, and vendor libraries still differ between
platforms, so a common PyTorch revision does not make the backends identical.

PyTorch uses \code{torch.compile} with default settings. Max-autotune,
GEMM and pointwise autotuning, and coordinate-descent tuning are off. The
compiler's and vendor libraries' own heuristics still apply
\cite{pytorch2026compile}. We did not use max-autotune because it was costly
and unreliable during bring-up. It compiled for a long time on H100, its
convolution searches failed on both Radeons, and it did not finish its first
ResNet check on Arc within an hour.\footnote{\href{https://github.com/kvark/inferena/blob/7b8fcb72e55410d8e33bbe948f89be50c691fcf7/EXPERIMENT.md}{Inferena protocol and bring-up notes, revision \code{7b8fcb72}}.}
A supervisor gives compilation 120 seconds for the first forward, backward,
and minimal-shape calls, and terminates the compiler on timeout. The engines
therefore prepare under different budgets. This is neither an equal-budget
contest nor a comparison with the best PyTorch result after hours of search.

CUDA and ROCm replay each phase through \path{torch.cuda.CUDAGraph}, which
ROCm maps to HIP graphs, and Intel through \path{torch.xpu.XPUGraph}
\cite{pytorch2026hip,pytorch2026xpugraph}. Inductor's own CUDA-graph
mechanism is disabled, so the explicit capture is the only replay. XPU
capture needs two recorded accommodations: the math scaled-dot-product
attention (SDPA) backend and an equivalent formulation of embedding backward
(Section~\ref{sec:availability}). MPS compiles every phase but has no public
whole-phase replay \cite{pytorch2026mpssource}. No failed compiled or
captured phase falls back to eager or CPU execution.

Table~\ref{tab:workloads} lists the five workloads. SmolLM2 uses pinned

(\path{name-index-uniform-v1}), not trained weights. Section~\ref{sec:scaling}
adds SmolLM2-360M and SmolLM2-1.7B.

Each process times three phases: a forward pass without gradients at the
full shape, a forward pass at a minimal shape, and forward--loss--backward
(F+L+B) without an optimizer update. For SmolLM2, the full forward is a
128-token prefill, and the minimal one is a single token without a key/value
cache, so it is not decode latency. The diffusion U-Net and Whisper already
run at batch one, so their minimal and full shapes coincide. Each phase warms
up for at least five calls and two seconds, then records 20 synchronized
host-wall samples. Input upload and output readback happen outside the timed
interval.

Each condition runs in three fresh process pairs, with alternating engine
order and rotating condition order. We report the median of the three
per-process medians and keep every sample and the range. A phase enters an
aggregate only with three qualified pairs, where \emph{qualified} means it
passed the gates of Section~\ref{sec:gates}. A failed backward pass does not
discard a qualified forward pass, and no eager run or older result fills a
missing one.

\subsection{Arithmetic contracts}
\label{sec:contracts}

Strict f32 disables TensorFloat-32 (TF32) in PyTorch and reduced-precision
cooperative matrices in \system{}. Storage, accumulation, and outputs stay
f32. Cooperative matrix tiles with f32 inputs and f32 accumulation remain
allowed, because they keep the arithmetic width, though not the reduction
order. Apple M3 exposes such tiles and uses them in all five workloads; none
of the Vulkan devices exposes them through this stack. The accelerated
contract lets PyTorch use TF32 and lets \system{} use cooperative matrices
with f16 inputs and f32 accumulation for forward matrix, convolution, and
attention work. \system{} keeps derivative regions at full precision under
both contracts, whereas PyTorch's TF32 also covers the backward pass. The two
permissions are not the same numerical format, and a device may have no
implementation that uses them. Neither contract uses quantization or f16
tensor storage.

\subsection{Numerical gates}
\label{sec:gates}

Every timed pair passes two independent checks. Across engines, forward
outputs must agree within 1\% relative L2 error over 256 evenly spaced
samples, and the scalar loss within 1\%. For training, the total gradient norm
and the vector of per-parameter gradient norms must agree within 5\% in
strict mode, after packed native gradients are restored to checkpoint
coordinates. The accelerated contract allows 10\% per process but requires
the median of the three processes to stay below 5\%. In practice, all 198
complete GPU pairs, including the larger models, and all 30 CPU-reference
qualifications stay below 5\%. The largest errors are 0.296\% for the
parameter-norm vector, 0.241\% for sampled outputs, and 0.0140\% for the
loss. Norms can miss sign or elementwise errors and samples can miss
localized ones, which is why the second check exists.

Within PyTorch, every output and gradient element must repeat across two
uncaptured calls, or eight for accelerated training. On CUDA, HIP, and XPU,
two consecutive graph replays must then reproduce the first uncaptured
result. Each tensor must satisfy maximum- and RMS-error bounds of
$10^{-6}+10^{-4}r$, where $r$ is the reference maximum or RMS. Strict
gradients are checked per parameter and as a whole, accelerated gradients as
a whole with a coefficient of $10^{-2}$. The retained reports record these
checks, but not every tensor element.

A classified numerical failure keeps the phases that already passed and lets
the campaign continue, while an unexplained fault stops it. When compiled
PyTorch fails, one fresh eager process with math attention and the same
weights, inputs, device, and contract becomes the reference for \system{}.
It must pass the same repeatability gates, and it never supplies a timing.

\subsection{Performance}
\label{sec:performance}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} gives every ratio, and Fig.~\ref{fig:ratios}
plots the strict ones. Across the six platforms, the median strict ratio is
1.27 for inference, 1.09 for the minimal shape, and 1.92 for training.
\system{} is faster in 7 of 30, 12 of 29, and 4 of 29 comparisons. Three of
the twelve minimal-shape wins repeat diffusion's inference wins, because its
shapes coincide. Under the accelerated contract, the medians are 2.00, 1.27,
and 2.61, with 6, 10, and 4 wins. PyTorch's RX~7900~XT Whisper minimal and
training times are missing under both contracts.

The platforms differ more than these medians suggest. On the RX~7900~XT,
\system{} is faster for SmolLM2, SmolVLA, and diffusion in every phase, and
for Whisper inference; its SmolLM2 and SmolVLA minimal shapes take 0.19 and
0.22 of PyTorch's time. On the Arc~B570, PyTorch wins every phase, with
strict inference ratios of 1.76--2.62 and training ratios of 2.50--5.07. On
the RTX~5070, \system{} wins strict diffusion and ResNet inference (0.91 and
0.74) and the SmolLM2 one-token forward (0.92), but training takes
1.60--2.40 times as long. The Windows RTX~3050 is similar, at 1.16--1.64 for
inference and 1.63--2.18 for training. We did not tune kernels for the H100
before collection, so it shows how the shared implementation transfers to a
datacenter GPU. Its strict training ratios are 1.49--3.43, and its
accelerated ones 2.42--7.36, largest for ResNet.

The accelerated contract helps PyTorch far more than \system{}. On NVIDIA
GPUs, TF32 speeds up PyTorch's matrix work in both directions, whereas
\system{}'s f16 cooperative path covers only forward regions and is not
exhaustively tuned. On the RTX~5070, PyTorch's SmolLM2 prefill falls from
6.08 to 2.73\,ms, while \system{}'s rises from 7.29 to 7.52\,ms. \system{}'s
accelerated prefill is slower than its strict prefill in six of the seven
size-study configurations (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}). On the RX~7900~XT and
B570, PyTorch's times do not change between contracts.

The M3 results are noisy in both engines. Process medians for \system{}'s
strict one-token SmolLM2 span 6.88--43.11\,ms, and those for PyTorch's strict
SmolVLA inference span 8.95--40.35\,ms; the machine may have slept during some
runs. Dropping every sample above three times its process median, for both
engines, removes only 5 of 3,600 samples and moves one aggregate by 2.5\%, so
the spread is not a few outliers. We keep all samples. The M3 ratios,
including diffusion's apparent wins, support only broad conclusions.

\subsection{Where the time goes}
\label{sec:gaps}

Wall time alone cannot divide a gap into command recording,
synchronization, and kernel efficiency. The one-token SmolLM2 forward still
allows a simple check. At batch one, it reads every f32 weight once, so its
time bounds the memory bandwidth it achieves. Against spec-sheet peaks,
\system{} reaches 53\% of peak bandwidth on the RTX~5070 and 57\% on the
RX~7900~XT at 135M, and 48\% and 70\% at 360M. PyTorch reaches 49\% and 68\%
on the RTX~5070, but only 11\% and 18\% on the RX~7900~XT. \system{}'s
one-token lead on that GPU therefore comes mostly from a slow PyTorch path,
not an unusually fast \system{} kernel. On H100 at 135M, \system{} uses 12\% and PyTorch
13\% of the 3.35\,TB/s peak, where fixed per-step costs dominate; such
dispatch overheads have been characterized for WebGPU across vendors
\cite{maczan2026dispatch}. At 1.7B, PyTorch
reaches 63\% and \system{} 29\%. Strict 1.7B prefill tells the same story
for compute: about 37 TFLOP/s in PyTorch and 15 in \system{}, against an FP32
peak of 67. For SmolLM2 on H100, the gap comes from the efficiency of large
matrix and matrix--vector kernels, not from a fixed per-dispatch cost that
larger models would amortize.

Matrix instruction access may add to the accelerated H100 gap. Hopper's CUDA
path issues asynchronous matrix operations across a 128-thread warpgroup
(\code{wgmma}) \cite{nvidia2026wgmma}. The Blade/Naga Vulkan path accepts only
subgroup-scope $8\times8$ or $16\times16$ tiles \cite{blade2026matrices} and
does not use \code{VK\_NV\_cooperative\_matrix2}, which adds workgroup scope
\cite{khronos2024cooperative2}. Measuring that contribution would require
inspecting matched-precision machine code on H100.

Performance portability requires the code to run first.
Table~\ref{tab:qualification} lists GPUs where one stack could not produce a

\code{RAPHAEL\_MENDOCINO}. On the Radeon~780M, ROCm needed
\code{HSA\_OVERRIDE\_GFX\_VERSION=11.0.2} and
\code{HSA\_ENABLE\_SDMA=0}, and the compiled campaign still failed on its
first strict condition with an unspecified HIP launch failure. \system{}
completed that condition and then passed all ten Vulkan qualifications
without any override. These results describe the configurations we tested,
not every possible PyTorch setup.

The RX~7900~XT exposes a numerical failure in compiled PyTorch 2.13.0 on
ROCm~7.2. All six Whisper training processes fail on the first uncaptured
repeat, before HIP graph capture. Identical calls differ by
$1.84$--$2.45\times10^{-3}$ at the worst output element, against a bound of
$9.10\times10^{-6}$, while captured inference passes in all six. The eager
math-attention diagnostic passes repeatability and agrees with \system{}'s
gradients within 0.001\% in the parameter-norm vector. That points at the
compiled PyTorch path; we have not isolated the faulty kernel.

On the Arc~B570, XPU graph capture needed the two accommodations of
Section~\ref{sec:reference-policy}. PyTorch's built-in dense embedding
backward matched only 19,968 of 73,728 elements in an exact shape-derived
probe, so the harness uses PyTorch's equivalent \code{index\_add}
formulation, which matches all of them. Fused SDPA failed capture with an
event-wait error, so XPU uses math SDPA. Neither changes a tolerance or
disables replay. At 360M, compilation finished in 89.62\,s, but training
capture failed in \path{aten.embedding_dense_backward} with an event-wait
error: passing the small probe did not guarantee larger models.

\system{} has its own gap. On the MI300X, PyTorch on ROCm ran strict 135M,
but no Vulkan driver stack produced a validated \system{} run. Mesa's RADV
supports graphics-capable GCN and RDNA GPUs, not this CDNA accelerator
\cite{mesa2026radv}. A custom driver and compute-queue trial reached
execution but failed on real workloads. Mesa issue 13399 tracks experimental
RADV support for CDNA \cite{mesa2025cdna}. It could open a Vulkan path to
MI300X, but it is not a driver we qualified. The obstacle is driver support,
not the tensor computation.

\subsection{What it explores}

Table~\ref{tab:search} counts the search in the main matrix: 504 sessions
tried 19,696 programs, a median of 45.5 of the 64 allowed. In 169 sessions (34\%), the chosen graph or schedule
differs from ordinary extraction, so the retained alternatives do get used.
The search is budget-bound. In 459 sessions (91\%) the deadline arrived before
every program was measured, the longest after 64.39\,s against the soft
60-second limit, and extraction was truncated in 396. Kernel probes visited
or reused 291,436 of 293,310 eligible kernel classes and accepted 7,133
replacements. They rejected 3,214 kernel outputs and 725 whole programs for
numerical mismatch, and a rejected candidate never replaces the checked
incumbent. The M3 kept the ordinary graph in 83 of its 84 sessions, which is
consistent with its noisy timing, since a challenger must beat the incumbent
by more than the measured noise. The B570 used no cooperative matrices: its
driver exposes rectangular $8\times16\times16$ tiles, and this Blade/Naga path
accepts only square ones \cite{blade2026matrices}.

\subsection{What it gains}

To see what the search buys, we reran \system{} alone on the host of the
RTX~5070 and Arc~B570, with the same revisions and drivers, under two
cheaper policies. \emph{No search} extracts one graph by estimated traffic
and uses heuristic kernel parameters. The \emph{2\,s tuner} is the library's
default optional tuner, which keeps the extracted graph and dispatch plan and
tunes kernel parameters for at most eight kernel classes within two seconds.
Each policy ran in three fresh processes per workload, with the same warmup
and sampling. Every output agreed with the main campaigns' PyTorch outputs
within the gates of Section~\ref{sec:gates}; the worst parameter-norm error
was 0.15\%. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} compares both policies with the
60-second search timings from the main campaigns; we did not rerun the search. The artifact holds
the small runner change that selects each policy, and every record.

The full search is where the gain is. Compared with no search, it speeds up
inference, the minimal shape, and training by geometric means of 1.39, 1.55,
and 1.23 on the RTX~5070, and 1.48, 1.23, and 1.18 on the B570. The gain is
concentrated in the transformer and diffusion workloads: SmolLM2, SmolVLA,
and the U-Net run 1.50--2.13$\times$ faster in inference, while ResNet and
Whisper inference gains at most 12\%. The two-second tuner gains 2--10\% on
average and $1.38\times$ at most. We have not separated how much of that difference
comes from the search's wider scope and how much from its longer budget. The
cost is clear, though: without search, a workload prepares in a median of
about two seconds; with it, in about two minutes.

The same holds at the kernel level. Consider four small matrix products
($50\times4096\times720$, $50\times960\times720$, $50\times720\times960$, and
the second with a fused add). Keeping tile and split-K alternatives as e-graph
equalities and timing whole steps beats extracting one graph and tuning its
kernels by 1.27--1.66$\times$ on the RTX~5070 and 1.23--1.63$\times$ on the
B570. In every case the search picked eight-way split-K. Preparation grows
from 0.4--0.7\,s to 6.2--7.1\,s on the RTX~5070, and from 0.8--1.8\,s to
7.6--11.7\,s on the B570. Here the e-graph's contribution is keeping such
schedules alive until they can be measured; an equally capable enumerator
could find the same ones.

\system{}'s preparation covers graph construction, optimization,
differentiation, pipeline creation, initialization, numerical qualification,
and search, for each session a workload needs (three, or two for Whisper).
PyTorch's covers compilation and the first call of each phase, with graph
capture and replay checks counted separately. Neither includes file loading
or process start-up. On H100, native preparation takes 72.81 minutes in
Table~\ref{tab:preparation}, against 16.86 for PyTorch's compilation. Per
workload, the strict process medians span 79.87--185.59\,s for \system{} and
2.19--83.95\,s for PyTorch, with medians of 127.90 and 26.73\,s, and every
PyTorch compilation stayed within its 120 seconds. At 1.7B on H100,
\system{} prepares for 211.06\,s strict and 216.69\,s accelerated, against
about 40\,s for PyTorch.

Little of that time measures the programs themselves. Of the 427 minutes the
main-matrix sessions spent searching, only 35 were the paired samples that
decide between two programs. Preparing each candidate from scratch took 180:
81 to allocate its session and compile its pipelines, 40 to upload its
weights, and 59 for full-tensor checks, since every comparison qualifies the
candidate three times and the incumbent once. Kernel probes took 122, and the
warmup of at least 250\,ms before each of the 18,152 comparisons took most of
the remaining 82.
Lowering graphs to WGSL took 0.56 minutes in total. Sharing immutable weights
with the incumbent, which the search interface already allows but the
benchmark runner does not use, caching pipelines across candidates, and
checking each program once would remove much of this overhead. A faster shader
compiler would not.

\section{Deployment Beyond the Matrix}
\label{sec:edge}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\columnwidth]{../figures/dinovision-quest3s.jpg}
  \caption{DinoVision's live RGB reconstruction on a Quest~3S, captured
  from the headset's cast.}
  \label{fig:dinovision}
\end{figure}

The case that motivates a graphics-API ML stack is an application that
already renders. DinoVision is one such application. It runs on the
Qualcomm Adreno GPU of a Meta Quest~3S under Android and reconstructs the
headset cameras' images from features of a DINOv3 vision encoder
\cite{simeoni2025dinov3} (Fig.~\ref{fig:dinovision}). \system{} trains the
2.01-million-parameter decoder on a desktop GPU with Adam and saves the
checkpoint. The headset loads it into a joined batch-one encoder/decoder
graph. The deployed graph performs 2.359 billion multiply--accumulates and
uses the same graph representation, compiler, memory planner, checkpoint
format, and Blade runtime as the benchmark. Two asynchronous eye sessions
share Blade's Vulkan context and queue with the OpenXR renderer.

We checked two things. First, for one fixed public RGB frame and identical
weights, the preprocessed patches are bit-exact between desktop and headset.
The encoder output agrees to a relative L2 error of 0.001059, and the final
reconstruction to 0.000457. Second, sharing the queue changes what the right
schedule is. In isolation, the graph runs in 96.78\,ms as one submission, and
splitting it into two chunks costs 4.1\%. During a 90-second live stereo
session, the two-chunk plan instead raises the application's render
submissions by 18.8\% and the slower eye's update rate by 8.7\%, and cuts
median worker latency by 20.2\%. Larger chunk counts help less, and
unevenly. The best plan for the renderer is not the best in isolation, and
one shared graphics stack lets the application choose it. This is a
deployment check, not an on-device training or PyTorch-on-Android
comparison. The weights, raw samples, manifests, and full-resolution media
are in the public
\href{https://huggingface.co/mad-bot/dinovision/tree/2c3f9017fe74c41482b165890c14737a2ccd4b6a}{DinoVision evidence archive},
and its \href{https://github.com/kvark/dinovision/tree/dc35cdf1c7c910cdd93c5b5362846842ae469a21}{source code}
is public too.

\section{Maintenance and Debugging}
\label{sec:productivity}

We did not measure developer effort, learnability, or maintenance time, and
we claim no quantitative productivity advantage. Some observations still
bear on it. Application code expresses tensor graphs and reuses checkpoints
across backends. One stack means one implementation to maintain, but
maintaining it takes WGSL, memory, synchronization, and numerical expertise.
A static compiler can fuse away or alias the values a developer wants to
inspect, so debugging may need a less optimized plan. \system{} can read
named intermediates, map graph operations to dispatches, locate the first
nonfinite value, and run the same kernels eagerly. These tools separate
rewrite, storage-reuse, and kernel bugs, although eager execution of the same
kernels is not an independent reference. Calibrated timestamps align CPU work
with GPU passes, while native tools such as Nsight Graphics remain necessary
for occupancy and stall analysis \cite{nvidia2026gputrace}. Deployment needs
no Python or vendor ML runtime, which kept the DinoVision integration within
one graphics stack.

\section{Related Work}

Pennycook et al.\ define and later revisit the portability metric used here
\cite{pennycook2016metric,pennycook2021revisiting}. Kokkos and RAJA express
portable parallelism and memory policy in C++ libraries
\cite{edwards2014kokkos,beckingsale2019raja}, and SYCL standardizes
single-source heterogeneous C++~\cite{khronos2020sycl}. We start instead from
graphics APIs and build a tensor compiler on top. Lin et al.\ measure
programming-model productivity through semantic divergence
\cite{lin2024productivity}; preparation time and executable size do not
replace that measure.

TVM, Glow, MLIR, and Triton established graph and kernel compilation for ML
\cite{chen2018tvm,rotem2018glow,lattner2021mlir,tillet2019triton}, and RAF
extends whole-graph compilation to training~\cite{yu2023raf}. TASO searches
verified graph substitutions \cite{jia2019taso}. TENSAT and Glenside apply
equality saturation to tensor programs
\cite{yang2021tensat,smith2021glenside}, building on egg
\cite{willsey2021egg}. \system{} uses egglog \cite{zhang2023egglog}, adds
matrix schedules to the same e-graph, and measures candidates instead of
trusting one cost model. Compile-time autotuning is itself established
practice \cite{chen2018tvm,tillet2019triton}.

Several systems already run ML through graphics APIs. IREE compiles MLIR
programs to Vulkan and Metal, and TinyIREE measures its footprint for
embedded inference \cite{liu2022tinyiree}. llama.cpp \cite{ggml2026llamacpp}
and ncnn \cite{tencent2026ncnn} run inference through Vulkan kernels, WebLLM
and LlamaWeb do so through WebGPU \cite{ruan2024webllm,levine2026llamaweb},
and ExecuTorch deploys exported PyTorch programs across mobile backends
\cite{nachin2026executorch}. TensorFlow.js trained models in the browser
\cite{smilkov2019tensorflowjs}, and memory-constrained on-device training
studies a complementary regime \cite{lin2022ondevice}. Burn is a broader Rust
framework with compute and graphics backends, autodiff, and fusion
\cite{simard2026burn}. Our contribution is a measured comparison of one
compact graphics-API stack for both training and inference against compiled
PyTorch across vendors, including its failures, preparation cost, and a
deployment. \inferena{} can drive several of these frameworks, but we compare
only against PyTorch, which trains on the GPUs of every vendor here.

\section{Threats to Validity}

\emph{Platforms.} A common PyTorch revision does not equalize vendor
libraries, drivers, host CPUs, or operating systems. The XPU accommodations
mean Intel runs a recorded variant of the stock path, and MPS has no
whole-phase replay.

\emph{Budgets.} The engines prepare under different policies and limits, so
we claim neither equal startup cost nor the fastest possible implementation.
The search study reruns only the two cheaper policies, within a day of
the main campaigns, on one host with two GPUs; its 60-second numbers come
from those campaigns.

\emph{Replication.} Three processes per condition do not characterize a
population of machines. CPU power management, cloud co-tenancy, thermals,
and the short timing window can all matter. Fresh processes start with empty
TorchInductor caches but keep whatever driver state the machine had. We
report warmed short-window latency rather than sustained throughput and
remove no outliers.

\emph{Numerics.} Strict f32 fixes arithmetic width, not operation order. The
cross-engine gates use sampled outputs, gradient norms, and one parameter
distribution, and PyTorch's full-element replay checks compare it only with
itself. The six RX~7900~XT Whisper training results validate \system{}
against the eager diagnostic, not the failed compiled reference.

\emph{Scope.} The larger models run at batch one with short contexts and no
key/value cache. The timed step excludes optimizer updates and communication,
and the H100 size study spans two cloud allocations. The diffusion U-Net is
reduced, Whisper lacks its decoder, and ResNet folds its normalization.
Experience with several vendors shaped this compiler, so the results do not
separate its architecture from the engineering effort behind it.

\section{Future Work}

The immediate work is to spend the search budget better: reuse initialized
weights and compiled pipelines across candidates, spend time on the kernels
that dominate, and rank candidates with measured kernel costs instead of
logical bytes. Since the two-second tuner keeps only a small part of the
search's gain, the goal is a budget of a few seconds that keeps most of it.
The B570 needs rectangular cooperative tiles, and
H100 needs faster large-matrix and matrix--vector kernels. Further out,
persistent megakernels could move dependency scheduling onto the GPU, as MPK
does for CUDA task graphs \cite{cheng2026mpk}. Graphics APIs raise their own
questions there, about workgroup forward progress, residency, and
cross-workgroup synchronization.

\section{Conclusion}

Graphics APIs are a workable path for ML deployment. \system{}, about 42k
lines of Rust in a 13.5\,MiB executable, trains and runs five workloads on six
GPUs from four vendors under Linux, Windows, and macOS. It passes the same
checks on three integrated GPUs where our PyTorch setups had no GPU path, and
runs inside an Android XR application next to its renderer. PyTorch is still
faster in most comparisons. \system{}'s median strict time is $1.27\times$
PyTorch's for inference and $1.92\times$ for training, with wins on the
RX~7900~XT and losses everywhere on the Arc~B570. Measured search accounts for
part of that performance. On two GPUs it is $1.18$--$1.55\times$ faster than
no search on average, at the price of about two minutes of preparation per
workload. Both stacks have gaps. Compiled PyTorch fails Whisper training on ROCm and
large-model capture on Arc, and \system{} has no Vulkan driver on MI300X, so
availability belongs beside speed when judging portability.
