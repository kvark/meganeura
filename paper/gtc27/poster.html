Training and Inference on NVIDIA GPUs Through Vulkan: Where the Gap to CUDA Lies

Dzmitry Malyshau · Independent Researcher · kvark@fastmail.com · github.com/kvark/meganeura

One 19 MiB Rust engine, compiled to Vulkan compute shaders, reaches CUDA PyTorch's strict-f32 speed for inference on RTX 5070. The remaining gap is in dense GEMM kernels, tensor-core use and host submission.

1Question

On NVIDIA hardware, machine learning means CUDA. The same GPUs also run Vulkan, with compute shaders, subgroups and tensor cores through VK_KHR_cooperative_matrix. Vulkan is already on nearly every GPU. How close does a compact engine get to CUDA PyTorch through that path, and where is the time lost?

2Meganeura

A compiler and runtime in about 54k lines of Rust. It differentiates tensor graphs and lowers them, with their gradients, to WGSL compute shaders. Graph rewrites and matrix schedules live in an e-graph, and the engine chooses among programs by timing them on the device it runs on.

3Method

  • GPUs: GeForce RTX 5070 (Blackwell) and H100 80GB HBM3, Linux, driver 595.91.07 for both Vulkan and CUDA.
  • Reference: PyTorch 2.13, torch.compile with CUDA graph replay.
  • Workloads: SmolLM2-135M, SmolVLA, Stable Diffusion U-Net, ResNet-50, Whisper-tiny. Each has a batch or prefill pass, a minimal-shape pass (one token), and a full training step (forward, loss, backward).
  • Strict f32: f32 inputs, accumulation and outputs; TF32 off.
  • Reduced precision: PyTorch autocast BF16. Meganeura may select F16/BF16 cooperative-matrix products, keeping parameters, loss and accumulation in f32.
  • Gates first: each engine must pass full-output and full-gradient checks before it is timed.
  • Timing: 10 s warmup, 20 samples, 3 processes. Ratio = Meganeura time / PyTorch time; below 1 is faster.

Driver finding

On driver 595, a dynamically indexed nested function-local array such as array<array<f32,4>,8> reads zero for outer indices at or above the inner length. Our 8×4 GEMM tile first looked fast because it dropped half its rows. A flat accumulator avoids it, and a raw-ash reproducer is reported.

With strict f32, both engines use the same arithmetic on the same CUDA cores, and Meganeura is at parity for RTX 5070 inference. It is faster for ResNet-50 (0.74) and the U-Net (0.80) and for single-token decoding (0.84). Training trails by 1.2–2.0× on the RTX 5070 and 1.2–3.0× on the H100. Whisper is the outlier: its long-sequence attention has no fused kernel here, giving 3.2× on the RTX 5070 and 7.4× on the H100 at reduced precision.

5Gap analysis on RTX 5070

  1. Dense f32 GEMM: 1.3–1.7× cuBLAS. With TF32 off, cuBLAS also runs CUTLASS SIMT kernels on CUDA cores, so this is a like-for-like kernel gap: 11.8 against 19.9 TFLOP/s at 4096³. A 128×64 tile with an 8×4 register block per thread cut it from 2.06×.
  2. Small long-K GEMMs: split-K. cuBLAS splits the K dimension. Adding split-K took 64×1024→256 from 4.40× to 1.88×; each split costs an extra reduction dispatch.
  3. 3×3 convolutions are faster. Winograd in three dispatches takes 0.34–0.64× of cuDNN's batch-1 time, once weight transforms are cached across steps.
  4. Tensor cores. 16×16 F16/BF16 cooperative matrices give native programs only 1.1–1.4×. This path has no TF32 inputs and no control of Hopper warpgroup MMA, TMA or clusters; attributing the H100 gap to them needs kernel-level profiles.
  5. Dispatch is at parity; submission is not. A dependent dispatch with a barrier costs 0.97 µs, against 0.85 µs per op inside a CUDA graph. Independent dispatches take 0.12 µs. One submit-and-wait takes 19.4 µs against 6.9 µs for an eager PyTorch op and synchronize.
